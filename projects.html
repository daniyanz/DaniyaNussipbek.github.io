J/P Snake Robot Base Station

June 2026—July 2026

P—01 / Most Recent
Project Description

This project was completed for the Biorobotics Lab led by Professor Howie Choset, under the guidance of my mentor, Yizhu Gu. The J/P Snake Base Station is a portable control and power system designed to support operation of the lab’s J/P snake robot, which is being developed for applications including space missions. The base station supplies the high-power electrical requirements of the robot while also providing the connection between the snake and the laptop used to control it.

My work on the project included reviewing the electrical system and verifying that the selected components matched the system requirements. I identified and corrected errors in the provided electrical flowcharts, determined which components and connectors were missing, and sourced the necessary parts. I then assembled and electrically tested the system to verify that the circuit operated as intended.

On the mechanical side, I created the CAD model for the enclosure and designed the top and bottom panels around the internal electrical components, connectors, ventilation, and mounting requirements. I also designed and 3D printed custom mounts to secure components that required additional support, including the regenerative clamp, regenerative resistor, and soft-start module. I prepared manufacturing files for the metal panels, incorporated engraved graphics into the panel design, laser engraved the panels, and completed the final mechanical assembly of the base station.

Electrical systems · CAD enclosure design · component sourcing · 3D printing · laser engraving · assembly & testing

Fireblight Detecting Robot

November 2025—May 2026

P—03
Project Overview

This robot was developed by our team, Fireblighters, for the 2026 Farm Robotics Challenge. The system was designed to autonomously navigate rows of an apple orchard, detect fire blight using a computer vision model trained on images of infected trees, and map the locations of detected infections. The project received the $10,000 AMIGA Innovation Award. The full presentation of the robot can be found here.

Open full presentation on YouTube ↗

My primary responsibilities focused on mechanical integration and simulation. I assembled the complete robot model in SolidWorks and converted the assembly into a URDF model for use in Gazebo navigation simulations. This involved defining the robot structure, joints, and coordinate relationships so that the simulated model accurately represented the physical system.

I also designed and fabricated a custom spraying mechanism that allowed the robot to physically mark trees after an infection was detected. The mechanism dispensed a grass-safe marking spray near the identified tree, providing a visible indication of the locations detected by the perception system.

Automatic Pill Box Project

24-101 · Introduction to Mechanical Engineering

November 2025—December 2025

C—03 / Class Project
Project Overview

The goal of this project was to design and fabricate an automated pill-dispensing mechanism. The system was designed to dispense three different marble balls, representing pills, at 60-second intervals. The concept was intended to address accessibility challenges faced by elderly users or individuals who may have difficulty opening conventional pill containers and organizing the correct dosage.

Our team designed the structural housing using woodworking techniques and built an internal rigid frame using aluminum T-slot extrusion to support the mechanical components. We applied principles of solid mechanics and gear relationships to work with a provided motor and design a gear-driven dispensing mechanism that met the required timing interval.

The project also emphasized practical engineering constraints. We worked within a fixed budget and a limited set of available materials, which required us to develop efficient designs that could be realistically fabricated and assembled. Through the project, I gained experience in woodworking, mechanical design, gear calculations, prototyping, fabrication, and designing within cost and material constraints.

Woodworking · T-slot framing · solid mechanics · gear calculations · prototyping · fabrication · design constraints

Repurposing an Electric Kettle Project

ME 270 · Design for Manufacturability

April 2025—May 2025

C—04 / Class Project
Project Overview

The goal of this project was to select a product that was no longer being used and redesign it for a new purpose. I chose to repurpose an electric kettle into a bird feeder while applying principles of design for manufacturability, circular economy, and cradle-to-cradle thinking.

I began by researching the original manufacturing processes used to produce the kettle and its individual components. I then generated ten possible repurposing concepts and used a Pugh matrix to evaluate the alternatives and select the strongest design direction. After selecting the bird feeder concept, I developed a product design specification to define the functional and design requirements.

To create the final product, I disassembled the kettle and reused its existing components in a new configuration, mounting the kettle upside down so it could dispense bird feed. The project also introduced me to engineering drawings and helped me better understand how product design decisions relate to manufacturing processes, material reuse, and end-of-life considerations.

Design for manufacturability · circular economy · cradle-to-cradle design · Pugh matrix · product design specification · engineering drawings

Handheld Vacuum Teardown & Manufacturing Analysis

ME 270 · Design for Manufacturability

February 2025—March 2025

C—05 / Class Project
Project Overview

In this project, our team reverse-engineered a handheld vacuum cleaner to understand how a consumer product is designed, manufactured, assembled, and brought to market. We fully disassembled the product and documented its internal and external components, including the housing, motor, battery, PCB, filter, valves, and accessories.

We created a bill of materials and analyzed which components were custom versus standard, the materials used, and the likely manufacturing processes for each part. This included examining evidence of processes such as injection molding and identifying how part geometry and material selection supported low-cost mass production.

We also estimated manufacturing cost and developed a process flow describing how raw materials and purchased components could move through sourcing, manufacturing, assembly, quality control, packaging, and distribution. The project helped me better understand design for manufacturability, product architecture, assembly strategy, manufacturing economics, and how engineering decisions affect high-volume consumer products.

Product teardown · bill of materials · injection molding · process planning · cost estimation · design for manufacturability
